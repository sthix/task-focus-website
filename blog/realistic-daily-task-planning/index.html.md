# Why Your To-Do List Never Fits Your Day

> How to plan more realistically: size the list to the hours you actually have, estimate from what really happened last time, and put one meaningful outcome first.

By Sascha Thiele, developer of Task Focus · Published 2026-10-12 · Canonical page: https://taskfocusapp.com/blog/realistic-daily-task-planning/

## The short answer

Your list doesn’t fit because it mixes everything you might do with what you can do today, and because people reliably underestimate their own tasks (the planning fallacy). Count your real available hours, estimate from similar past tasks, leave room for interruptions, and commit to one useful outcome first.

Every morning the list looks reasonable. Twelve items, a good day’s work. By evening, four are done, two are half-done, and the rest move to tomorrow, which already had its own twelve. After a few weeks, the list stops feeling like a plan and starts feeling like a verdict.

The problem usually isn’t effort. It’s that the list was never sized to the day. Here’s how to plan a day that actually fits, and how to get better at it every evening.

## Capturing everything isn’t the same as committing to today

A to-do list often does two jobs at once. It holds **everything you might need to do**, so you don’t forget, and it’s supposed to say **what you’ll do today**. Those need different lists.

- **The inventory** can be as long as it needs to be. It’s a storage place, not a promise.
- **Today’s commitment** should be small enough that finishing it is realistic on an ordinary day, interruptions included.

Most of the frustration comes from treating the inventory as the commitment.

## Why we keep overfilling the day

Psychologists call it the **planning fallacy**. In classic studies by Buehler, Griffin and Ross, people consistently underestimated how long their own tasks would take, even when they knew similar tasks had run late before. When predicting, they focused on how the future task would go and largely ignored their own past experience.[1]

The same research offers the way out. When people were prompted to connect their past experience to their prediction, the optimistic bias disappeared in one of the studies. In plain terms: stop asking “how long should this take?” and ask “how long did the last one like it actually take?”

## A realistic plan in four steps

### 1. Count the hours you actually have

Start with your working day, then subtract what’s already spoken for: meetings, commuting, lunch, school pickup, the admin that always happens. Many people find their “eight-hour day” has three or four hours of real working time.

### 2. Estimate from past examples

For each candidate task, think of the last similar task you finished and how long it really took, not how long you hoped it would. If you have no record, a rough honest guess is still better than an optimistic one.

### 3. Leave room for the day you’ll actually have

Interruptions aren’t surprises; they’re part of the day. Look at a typical day last week: how much time went to things you didn’t plan? Leave about that much space. There’s no universal percentage. Your own track record is the best guide.

### 4. Choose one useful, achievable outcome

Before you fill the rest of the day, decide what would make it a good day if it were the only thing you finished. Put it first. Then add smaller items only if there’s time left in the estimate.

## Worked example: an overfilled Tuesday

Here’s a real-looking list from someone with two meetings and roughly four hours of open time:

| Original list | Honest estimate | Decision |
|---|---|---|
| Draft the project proposal | 3 hours (last one took 3½) | Split: write the outline and the problem section today (1½ h) |
| Reply to client emails | 45 min | Keep, after lunch |
| Review the design mockups | 1 hour | Keep |
| Update the budget sheet | 1 hour | Move to Thursday |
| Book dentist appointment | 10 min | Keep, between meetings |
| Clean up the shared drive | 2 hours | Back to the inventory |
| Read the industry report | 1 hour | Back to the inventory |

The original list needed almost nine hours. The plan now needs about three and a half, leaving some room for the unplanned. The one outcome that makes the day good: **the proposal outline and problem section are written**.

Notice that nothing was deleted. Two items went back to the inventory, one moved to a specific day, and the biggest task was split into a piece that can actually be finished today.

## End the day by comparing estimate and reality

This is the step that makes tomorrow’s plan better. Before you stop, take two minutes:

****A two-minute evening check****

- What did I finish?
- For the main task, how long did I estimate, and how long did it take?
- What came up that I didn’t plan for?

After a week, you’ll have a small, honest record of how long things take you and how much of your day disappears to interruptions. That record is what step 2 and step 3 need.

It’s worth being modest about what any planning method can promise. A large 2021 meta-analysis of 158 studies found moderate relationships between time management and both performance and well-being, but much of that evidence was correlational and didn’t validate any single daily formula.[2] The routine above is a sensible starting point, not a proven recipe. Adjust it to your days.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is my to-do list always too long?

Usually because it mixes everything you might do with what you can do today, and because people tend to underestimate how long their own tasks take. Separate the inventory from today’s commitment, and estimate from past experience.

### How many tasks should I plan per day?

There’s no universal number. Size the plan to your real available hours, minus time for interruptions, and make sure one meaningful outcome comes first.

### What is the planning fallacy?

It’s the tendency to underestimate how long your own future tasks will take, even when similar tasks have run late before. Basing estimates on how long comparable past tasks actually took reduces it.
