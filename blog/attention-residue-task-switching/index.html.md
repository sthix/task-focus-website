# Why You Keep Thinking About the Last Task

> A quick check of your messages rarely costs only the seconds it takes. Part of your attention stays behind. Here’s why, and how to leave tasks more cleanly.

By Sascha Thiele, developer of Task Focus · Published 2026-10-12 · Canonical page: https://taskfocusapp.com/blog/attention-residue-task-switching/

## The short answer

Attention residue is the part of your attention that stays on a previous task after you switch, especially when that task was left unfinished. Reduce avoidable switches, and before necessary ones, write a short return note: where you stopped, your next action, and when you’ll come back.

You’re halfway through writing something. You glance at your messages, just quickly. There’s a question from a colleague, so you answer it. Then you go back to the document and stare at it. You’ve read the last paragraph three times and you’re still thinking about the message.

That lingering pull has a name: **attention residue**. It explains why a “quick check” rarely costs only the seconds it takes, and it points to two practical fixes: switch less often when you have the choice, and leave cleaner when you don’t.

## What attention residue is

The term comes from organisational researcher Sophie Leroy. In a 2009 paper, she reported two experiments in which people moved from one task to another. When the first task was left unfinished, part of their attention stayed with it, and they performed worse on the next task.[1]

Put simply: your mind doesn’t switch as cleanly as your screen does. When you leave something open, a portion of your thinking stays behind to keep track of it.

One surprising detail is that finishing the first task wasn’t always enough to clear the residue; the conditions under which people finished mattered too. Completion helps, but how you leave a task matters as well.

## Avoidable switches and necessary ones

Not every switch is a mistake. Some interruptions are part of the job: a meeting starts, a customer calls, your child needs you. Others are self-made: checking the inbox “just in case,” opening a feed during a pause in thinking, glancing at a notification that could have waited an hour.

- **Avoidable switches** are worth reducing. Each one leaves a little residue for no real gain.
- **Necessary switches** can’t be removed, but you can make them cheaper by leaving the first task in a clear state.

You may have seen a precise “minutes to refocus” figure quoted online. The research covered here doesn’t establish one universal number, and the real cost likely varies by person and task. What is consistent is the direction: leaving work unfinished and unplanned makes the next thing harder.

## The fix for necessary switches: a ready-to-resume plan

In a later study, Leroy and Theresa Glomb tested a simple intervention. Before switching to an interrupting task, people took a moment to note where they were and how they’d resume the original work. Under the conditions they studied, this reduced attention residue and helped people perform better on the interrupting task.[2]

The idea is that once your mind knows the unfinished work is “parked” safely, it doesn’t need to keep holding on to it.

## Try a three-line return note

Here’s a practical version you can use any time you have to leave something unfinished. It’s an adaptation of the research idea, not a copy of the study’s method. Before you switch, write three lines:

****The return note****

1. **Where I stopped:** the exact point you left off.
2. **Next action:** the very first thing to do when you come back.
3. **When I’ll return:** a time or a cue.

A worked example, written thirty seconds before a meeting:

> Stopped mid-way through section 2, after the pricing paragraph. Next: write the paragraph comparing the two options. Back at 2:30, after the team call.

Paper works. So does your usual notes app. The point isn’t the tool; it’s that you made the decision before leaving, so your attention doesn’t have to keep making it during the meeting.

## Fewer avoidable switches

For the self-made switches, prevention beats recovery. A few things help:

- **Batch your checking.** Choose set times for email and messages, such as before lunch and at the end of the day, rather than checking whenever there’s a pause.
- **Quiet what comes in.** An iPhone Focus can hold back notifications from everyone except the people who truly need to reach you.
- **Make the easiest escape a bit harder.** Most self-made switches happen through the phone, in a second, without a decision. Pausing those apps while you work changes that.

## Single-tasking is a practice, not a personality

Nobody works on only one thing all day. The goal isn’t perfect focus, it’s fewer pointless switches and cleaner exits from the necessary ones. A good day might still include a dozen task changes. What changes is that each one is a decision, and you leave something behind that tells you how to come back.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is attention residue in simple terms?

It’s the part of your attention that stays stuck on a previous task after you switch to a new one, especially when the previous task was left unfinished. It can make you slower or less accurate on the new task.

### How do I stop thinking about my last task?

Before switching, write down where you stopped, your next action, and when you’ll return. That gives your mind a reason to let go. When you have the choice, finish a natural chunk of the first task before switching.

### Is task switching the same as multitasking?

Mostly, yes. What feels like doing two things at once is usually rapid switching between them. Each switch has a cost, and attention residue is one part of that cost.
