Getting started

How to Start a Task When You Keep Putting It Off

When a task has sat on your list for days, the problem is rarely motivation. It’s usually that you can’t act on it directly, and there’s no clear moment to begin.

By Sascha ThielePublished October 12, 20265 min read

The short answer

Rewrite the task as one visible first action, such as “draft the opening paragraph” instead of “finish my essay”, then attach it to a reliable cue with an if-then sentence: “When I sit down after lunch, I will draft the opening paragraph.” Research on if-then plans shows a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals.

You know what needs doing. It has been on your list for days, maybe weeks. Every time you think about it, you feel a small drop in your stomach and find something else to do first. Then something else. Then it’s evening.

This isn’t usually a motivation problem. More often, the task is written as an outcome you can’t act on directly, and there’s no clear moment when you’re supposed to begin. Fix those two things and starting gets much easier.

A goal isn’t something you can start

“Finish my essay” is a goal. You can’t do it right now, at your desk, in the next ten minutes. When your brain looks at it, it sees the whole thing at once: the research, the structure, the blank page. Avoiding that is a reasonable reaction.

An action is something you could see yourself doing. “Draft the opening paragraph” is an action. So is “open the essay document and paste in my three best quotes.” The test is simple: could someone watching you tell when you’d started, and when you’d finished?

GoalFirst action
Finish my essayDraft the opening paragraph
Get ready for the interviewWrite answers to three likely questions
Clean up the garageClear the shelf by the door
Sort out my health insurance claimFind the receipt and photograph it

Decide when, not just what: if-then plans

Once you have an action, attach it to a moment. Psychologists call this an implementation intention, or more simply an if-then plan: “When situation X happens, I will do Y.”

The evidence for this is unusually strong. A 2006 meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that forming if-then plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (an average effect size of d = .65).1 That is a statistic across groups of people, not a promise about any one person, but it’s a good reason to try.

A separate line of lab research found that making a specific plan for an unfinished goal reduced how much that goal intruded on people’s thoughts during other tasks.2 In other words, deciding when you’ll do something can help you stop worrying about it in the meantime.

Write your own if-then sentence

A good plan has a reliable cue and a concrete action:

“When I sit down after lunch, I will draft the opening paragraph.”

Choose a cue that actually happens every day and is easy to notice: finishing a meal, arriving at the library, closing your laptop after a meeting, sitting down with the first coffee. Avoid vague cues like “when I have time” or “this afternoon.” They never quite arrive.

Some more examples:

  • Study: “When I get to the library, I will do the first five practice questions before opening anything else.”
  • Work: “When my 10 o’clock call ends, I will write the three-line summary for the client.”
  • Home admin: “When I finish breakfast on Saturday, I will find last year’s tax return and put it on the desk.”

Write the sentence down. Saying it in your head is easy to forget; seeing it written makes it a decision.

Make the first step smaller than seems necessary

If you still don’t start, the action is probably too big. Shrink it until starting feels almost silly. Not “write the report,” but “write the headings.” Not “do the dishes,” but “clear the counter by the sink.”

There isn’t a scientifically optimal size for a first step. This is practical advice, not a formula. But the smaller the step, the less there is to avoid, and finishing a small step often makes the next one easier to pick up.

Remove the easiest escape route

For most people, the easiest escape from an uncomfortable task is the phone. It takes one second and requires no decision. If that’s your pattern, close the exit before you begin.

This is where Task Focus fits. When your cue arrives, type the first action you wrote down, pick the apps you usually escape into, and begin. If you reach for one of them, you see the task you chose instead of the feed. When you’ve done the step, mark it done and the apps come back. Task Focus doesn’t write if-then plans for you. The sentence is your part; the app just protects the moment you chose.

When the plan breaks, change the plan

Plans meet real days. The call runs over, the library is closed, a child gets sick. That is not failure; it’s information.

It’s also worth knowing that very specific plans have a cost. Research by Masicampo and Baumeister found that people who made a detailed plan sometimes became less open to better alternatives that came up along the way.3 So hold the plan firmly enough to start, and loosely enough to change. If a cue doesn’t work twice, pick a different one.

Putting things off isn’t a character flaw

People delay tasks for many reasons: the task is unclear, it’s unpleasant, it’s tied to something stressful, they’re tired, or starting is genuinely hard for them. Some of that won’t be solved by a better sentence. If you find starting is persistently difficult across your whole life, that is worth discussing with a professional. An app and a planning trick aren’t a treatment for ADHD or anything else.

But for the ordinary, stuck-on-one-thing kind of delay, a concrete action and a clear moment to begin go a long way.

Frequently asked questions

How do I start a task when I’m overwhelmed?

Ignore the whole task for now and choose only the first visible action, something you could finish in 10 to 15 minutes. Attach it to a specific moment today with an if-then sentence, and start there.

What is an example of an implementation intention?

“When I sit down after lunch, I will draft the opening paragraph of my essay.” It names a situation (sitting down after lunch) and a concrete response (drafting the opening paragraph).

What should I do right after finishing the first step?

Take a short break, then choose the next step, written the same way. If your day is already crowded, our guide on planning a day that actually fits helps decide what deserves the next slot.

One small finish line

Write one concrete first step.

Then put that task into Task Focus for your next session, and let the apps you usually escape into wait.
